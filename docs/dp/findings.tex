\section{Motivation}

\subsection{The gap between a DP library and a DP system}

Differentially private aggregation is solved as an \emph{algorithm} and unsolved as a
\emph{product}. Every mature library --- Google DP, OpenDP, Tumult, PipelineDP --- can answer
\texttt{SELECT g, SUM(v) FROM T GROUP BY g} with a rigorous user-level guarantee. But none of them
can answer it \emph{without a human first supplying constants that depend on the data}:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{How much can one user contribute to one group?} A clipping bound. Set it too low and
you destroy the signal; too high and you drown it in noise.
\item \textbf{How many groups can one user touch?} \texttt{max\_partitions\_contributed}, called
$\Cu$ here. A required input in Google DP, with no default. Wilson et
al.~\cite{wilson2020} do provide a mechanism for inferring bounds automatically (their
\textsc{ApproxBounds}, \S5.1.1) --- but it infers the \emph{value} range $[L,U]$, and their
aggregate signatures are correspondingly \texttt{ANON\_SUM(col, L, U)}. $\Cu$ appears only as
``an engine-defined constant'' in their sensitivity theorem, with the paper stating: \emph{``For the
rest of this paper, we assume that contribution bounds are specified as literals in each query.''}
\end{itemize}

Our system rewrites ordinary SQL into a private plan automatically. An analyst writes a query; they
do not know --- and should not need to know --- the maximum number of months any customer appears
in. So every one of these constants is a blocker, and the question driving this work is narrow and
practical: \textbf{can $\Cu$ be chosen from the data itself, privately, without spending so much
budget that the answer becomes useless?}

\subsection{Guessing is not a safe fallback}

Two measured facts rule it out. The penalty is \emph{severely asymmetric} --- setting $\Cu$ too
small costs up to $38\times$ in error while setting it too large costs at most $1.7\times$ --- and
no single value works across datasets:

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{lrrrrr}
\toprule
analyst supplies & $\Cu{=}1$ & $\Cu{=}10$ & $\Cu{=}100$ & $\Cu{=}$ schema max & automatic \\
\midrule
worst case over queries & 177.6$\times$ & 136.3$\times$ & 5.18$\times$ & 4.18$\times$ &
\textbf{1.36$\times$} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

$\Cu{=}1$ is catastrophic on TPC-H and simultaneously \emph{optimal} on ClickBench. A single
constant cannot serve both, so the choice must adapt to the data --- which means spending privacy
budget to make it. The mechanism below spends about $1\%$ of $\epsilon$ doing so.

\subsection{Where the budget goes}

A grouped DP query spends privacy budget on three things:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Bound selection} ($\epsilon_b$): how large is one user's contribution?
\item \textbf{Partition selection} ($\epsilon_\eta$): which groups may be named at all? A group
existing because of a single user must not be revealed, so groups are released only when a noised
count of contributing users clears a threshold $\tau$.
\item \textbf{Values} ($\epsilon_v$): the noised aggregates themselves.
\end{enumerate}

The third is what the analyst wants; the first two are overhead.

\paragraph{How the baseline divides it.} Google DP charges these separately, though not as three
co-equal shares. Privacy on Beam splits the top level \emph{two} ways --- \texttt{count.go} exposes
\texttt{AggregationEpsilon} and \texttt{PartitionSelectionEpsilon} as distinct fields, consumed by
distinct calls --- and bound selection sits \emph{inside} the aggregation: \texttt{ApproxBounds} is
constructed with its own \texttt{epsilon} taken from the aggregation's share. We model all three as
a single three-way split and tune it jointly for every arm, which is at least as favourable to the
baseline as its own nested structure, since a nested split cannot beat a free one.

\paragraph{Scoring.} Throughout: relative $\ell_1$ error over the \emph{true, uncapped} key set,
with $\tau$-suppressed groups charged as released $0$ (full error). Every arm is tuned separately
over its own free parameters. Anything less lets a mechanism buy accuracy by returning fewer rows,
or flatters one side by fixing a parameter the other gets to optimise.

\section{The mechanism}

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Core idea.} The bound we need is a property of one \emph{distribution} --- how many groups
each user touches. Rather than measuring that distribution privately and reading a bound off it, we
turn the problem into a \emph{choice among candidate bounds}, scored by a quantity that a single
user can move by at most one. Choosing is far cheaper than measuring, and the asymmetry of the
error curve tells us which way to lean when the choice is imperfect.
\end{quote}

\subsection{In words}

Ask each user how many groups they appear in. On a monthly sales query one customer might appear in
30 months, another in 3, a few in 70. Lay those counts out as a distribution and $\Cu$ is a cut
point on it: choose it, and every user above the line has their extra groups discarded.

The mechanism sets that cut point at a \emph{quantile} --- ``pick $\Cu$ so that $95\%$ of users are
left untouched'' --- and it does so privately, because the distribution is itself sensitive data.
Three design choices make this work.

\paragraph{Aim at a rank, not a value.} Rather than asking ``what is the largest $\ku$?'', which one
user can move arbitrarily, we ask ``at what cut point do we reach $95\%$ of users?''. That target is
a \emph{count} of users, and one user changes any count by at most one. The bound is thus pinned to
a robust feature of the distribution, not to its most extreme member.

\paragraph{Choose, do not measure.} The obvious approach is to release the whole distribution
noisily and then read the quantile off it. That is expensive: one user with $\ku = 1$ shifts every
cumulative count at once, so publishing the full curve requires noise proportional to its length.
Instead we never publish the curve. We \emph{score} each candidate cut point by how close it lands
to the target rank, and draw one candidate at random with better candidates exponentially more
likely. Only the winner is released. Since each individual candidate's score moves by at most one
when a user is added or removed, the whole selection costs the same as measuring a single count ---
about $1\%$ of the query's budget in practice.

\paragraph{Deliberately overshoot.} A quantile is only a proxy for the bound that actually minimises
error, so the two will not coincide. That is tolerable because the error curve is lopsided:
undershooting discards real user data and costs up to $35\times$, while overshooting merely adds
noise and costs at most $2\times$ (\S\ref{sec:asym}). The target fraction $p$ is therefore set high
--- $0.95$ or $0.99$ rather than the $0.7$ one might first reach for --- so the mechanism errs on
the safe side of a curve that punishes one direction far more than the other.

Because the resulting bound depends only on the shape of the $\ku$ distribution, and that shape is
stable under filters on the user (\S\ref{sec:freeze}), the released value can be frozen and reused
across many later queries at no further cost.

\subsection{Formally}

Let $\ku = |\{g : u \text{ contributes to } g\}|$ and $F_d(r) = |\{u : \ku \le r\}|$, the number of
users whose group count is at most $r$. Given a public upper bound $K \ge \ku$ and a target
fraction $p$:

\begin{enumerate}
\item Release $\tilde N = N + \mathrm{Lap}(1/\epsilon_N)$, the number of users, at cost
$(\epsilon_N, 0)$. Its sensitivity is $1$.
\item Form a conservative target rank $T = p\tilde N + (p/\epsilon_N)\ln(1/2\beta_N)$; the margin
ensures $T \ge pN$ with probability $1-\beta_N$, so noise in $\tilde N$ does not silently pull the
target below its intended quantile.
\item Score each candidate $r \in \{1,\dots,K\}$ by $q(d,r) = -|F_d(r) - T|$: a candidate scores
well when the number of users it leaves untruncated is close to the target.
\item Sample by exponential mechanism, $\Pr[\hat\Cu = r] \propto \exp(\epsilon_C\, q(d,r)/2)$, at
cost $(\epsilon_C, 0)$. The histogram, the cumulative counts and the scores all remain internal;
only $\hat\Cu$ is released.
\end{enumerate}

Total $(\epsilon_N + \epsilon_C, 0)$-DP by adaptive sequential composition. $\hat\Cu$ is then
frozen and reused as public metadata.

\subsection{What we changed}

The construction above is Dandan's; steps 1--4 and the $\Delta q \le 1$ argument are hers. Our
contribution to the mechanism itself is three modifications, each forced by a measurement.

\paragraph{The target fraction, $0.7 \to 0.95$/$0.99$.} The original proposal suggested
$p = 0.7$ as a reasonable default. Measured across nine queries, that is the single worst choice
tested: it costs $17.75\times$ in the worst case, against $1.36\times$ at $p = 0.99$. The reason is
the asymmetry of \S\ref{sec:asym} --- at $p = 0.7$ the selected bound lands \emph{below} the
error-optimal one, on the steep side of the curve. The instinct to err high was right; the data
says to go much further.

\paragraph{The right $p$ depends on the surrounding mechanism.} A single value does not serve both
architectures. Where $\Cu$ is shared with the partition-selection threshold --- the published
baseline's structure --- a large $\Cu$ inflates $\tau$ as well, and chasing a heavy tail backfires:
$p = 0.99$ costs $2.46\times$ against $2.31\times$ at $p = 0.95$. Where the value and vote bounds
are separate, $p = 0.99$ wins outright ($1.36\times$ against $2.91\times$). This interaction was not
part of the original proposal and changes the recommendation depending on where the mechanism is
deployed.

\paragraph{When to re-select rather than reuse.} The proposal freezes $\hat\Cu$ and reuses it. We
find that is correct for filters on the privacy unit --- where the $\ku$ distribution is
\emph{identical} even after discarding $99\%$ of users --- but wastes $1.8\times$ on filters that
remove whole groups, where $\ku$ collapses and re-selection pays for itself. The decidable test is
in \S\ref{sec:freeze}, and it is not simply ``does the \texttt{WHERE} clause touch the grouping
key''.

\paragraph{Two safety modifications} were needed for the mechanisms this one feeds
(\S\ref{sec:privacy}): a clip written as a signed sum rather than a norm, and a $\delta$ that did
not cover every group a user can create. Neither is in the selection mechanism itself, but both
must be fixed in anything that consumes its output.

\subsection{Why the score sensitivity is $1$, not $K$}

This is what makes step 4 cheap. Removing one user with $\ku = k$ shifts $K-k+1$ cumulative counts
\emph{simultaneously}, so \emph{releasing} the vector $(F(1),\dots,F(K))$ would have $\ell_1$
sensitivity up to $K$. But the exponential mechanism never releases that vector --- it scores each
candidate separately, and what matters is $\Delta q = \max_r |q(d,r) - q(d',r)|$. Verified by
explicit construction:

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{lrrr}
\toprule
removed unit & cumulative counts changed & $\ell_1$ change & $\max_r |\Delta q|$ \\
\midrule
$\ku = 1$  & 80 of 80 & 80 & \textbf{1.0} \\
$\ku = 30$ & 51 of 80 & 51 & \textbf{1.0} \\
$\ku = 70$ & 11 of 80 & 11 & \textbf{1.0} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The $\ell_1$ of the histogram is a red herring: one cheap release replaces $K$ noisy counts.

\subsection{Multiple aggregates}\label{sec:multiagg}

Real queries request several measures at once, which splits $\epsilon_v$ $c$ ways while the
partition-selection cost is paid once. Measured on \texttt{month|nation} with up to four aggregates
(SUM price, SUM quantity, COUNT, SUM discount):

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{lrrrr}
\toprule
aggregates $c$ & 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 \\
\midrule
baseline & 13.52\% & 18.93\% & 22.86\% & 26.25\% \\
$\ell_1$ clip & 3.89\% & 8.02\% & 11.40\% & 13.40\% \\
\midrule
advantage & 3.48$\times$ & 2.36$\times$ & 2.01$\times$ & \textbf{1.96$\times$} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The clip's advantage decays and appears to flatten near $2\times$: $\tau$ is a fixed cost paid once
regardless of $c$, so as $c$ grows both arms converge toward pure value-channel noise where the gap
is just the sensitivity ratio. \textbf{Single-aggregate figures should therefore be read as the
upper end of a range.}

The selection mechanism is unaffected: $\Cu$ is chosen once per query, so its
$\epsilon_N + \epsilon_C$ is paid once regardless of $c$ and its relative cost stays at $\approx 1\%$
of the query budget. (Reasoned, not measured --- the experiment above varies the value channel, not
the selection.)

\subsection{Freeze once, or re-select per query?}\label{sec:freeze}

A single frozen $\Cu$ suffices for filters on the \emph{privacy unit}; only group-removing filters
justify re-selection. Frozen $\Cu = 48$ from the unfiltered query; per-query re-runs the mechanism
and pays $0.01$ again each time:

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{lrrrrrr}
\toprule
query & units & $\ku$ p50 & p95 & per-query $\Cu$ & frozen & per-query \\
\midrule
PU-side: acctbal $\ge$ 8000 & 181,532 & 30 & 48 & 48 & \textbf{12.36\%} & 12.56\% \\
PU-side: acctbal $\ge$ 9500 &  45,320 & 30 & 48 & 53 & \textbf{85.14\%} & 87.16\% \\
PU-side: acctbal $\ge$ 9900 &   9,182 & 30 & 48 & 57 & 100.0\% & 99.99\% \\
GRP-side: ship $\ge$ 1998   & 821,108 & \textbf{4} & \textbf{8} & \textbf{8} & 2.52\% & \textbf{1.38\%} \\
GRP-side: nation $<$ 5      & 199,738 & 30 & 48 & 48 & \textbf{2.31\%} & 2.40\% \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

A filter on the privacy unit removes users, but the survivors still touch the same number of groups
--- the $\ku$ distribution is \emph{identical} (p50 $=30$, p95 $=48$) even at a filter retaining
9,182 of 1,000,000 users. Re-selection returns the same value and is slightly worse for paying its
cost again. A filter that removes \emph{groups} collapses $\ku$ (p50 $30\to4$), and there
re-selection wins $1.8\times$.

Note that \texttt{nation < 5} constrains the grouping key yet behaves like a unit-side filter,
because nation is functionally determined by the customer. The decidable rule is therefore:

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Re-run the selection when the filter constrains a grouping-key component that is \emph{not}
functionally determined by the privacy unit. Otherwise reuse the frozen value.}
\end{quote}

\section{Privacy validation}\label{sec:privacy}

Beyond the $\Delta q \le 1$ construction above, we ran an explicit adversary against the full
pipeline, $4\times10^6$ trials per attack, measuring the advantage
$\Pr[\text{say } D'\,|\,D'] - \Pr[\text{say } D'\,|\,D]$ against the ceiling
$(e^\epsilon-1)/(e^\epsilon+1)$.

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
attack & result & verdict \\
\midrule
value channel, worst-case target & advantage 0.2587 vs bound 0.2913, \emph{scale-free} & pass \\
omniscient attacker (knows all other units) & 0.2590 even at a target contributing $4B$ & pass \\
reconstruction of a target's value & posterior sd 1,167 vs prior 1,183 ($1.01\times$) & pass \\
reuse of one frozen set over $N$ queries & advantage tracks composed $\epsilon_v$ only & pass \\
adaptive: round 1 picks the round-2 target & \emph{loses}, $0.36\times$ to $0.01\times$ & pass \\
SQL join fan-out (182 rows per unit) & $\|\Delta\|_1 = 1.000000\,B$ exactly & pass \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Two findings came out of this, both about \emph{our own} mechanisms, and both surfaced by explicit
neighbour construction after the informal argument had already convinced us.

\paragraph{Finding 1: a clip that was not a norm.} Written as $n_u = \sum_g \min(t,B)$ it is a signed
sum, which fails open on signed measures. On \texttt{SUM(shipped - returned)} at sf10,
\textbf{393,053 of 999,982 units exceeded the bound}, worst $2.6\times$ --- making the release
$2.6\epsilon$-DP. Fixed by $n_u := \sum_g|\mathrm{clip}(t,-B,B)|$, bit-identical on non-negative
data.

\paragraph{Finding 2: $\delta$ was under-charged, and an adversary confirms it.} A unit creates a group by
having a \emph{value} there, not a vote, so with votes truncated to $\Ce$ the $\ku-\Ce$ unvoted
groups each get an independent chance to clear $\tau$ on noise alone --- chances $\tau$'s union
bound never covered:

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{rrrrr}
\toprule
$\Ce$ & $\ku$ & leak, no gate & leak, gated & over by \\
\midrule
1 & 30 & $2.10\times10^{-5}$ & $1.00\times10^{-6}$ & \lose 21.0$\times$ \\
1 & 72 & $4.70\times10^{-5}$ & $1.25\times10^{-6}$ & \lose \textbf{47.0$\times$} \\
5 & 72 & $1.27\times10^{-5}$ & $1.75\times10^{-6}$ & \lose 12.8$\times$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The fix is one line --- release a group only if it has at least one vote --- and it is free: on real
data it removes 19 of 2,095 groups, each with a $2\times10^{-7}$ chance of release.

\paragraph{Refreshes must be budgeted.} $R$ re-releases of frozen metadata give $R$ independent
chances: $\Pr[\text{exposed}] = 1-(1-p)^R \le R\,\delta_0$, confirmed exactly at $R = 1..50$.
Batching updates does not reduce the cost of a single refresh; it reduces $R$.

\section{Experimental practice}\label{sec:method}

Several headline figures in this project were produced and later invalidated
($880\times \to 12\times \to 4.8\times \to 4.65\times \to 1.36\times$ for the clip, and separately
$4.5\times \to 1.9\times$ for the stack), in every case because a baseline had been left at a
default while our own arm was tuned. That is why every number here is reported against a baseline
tuned over its own free parameters, and why suppressed groups are charged full error. Three further
practices were needed, each learned from a result that turned out to be an artifact:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Verify sensitivity by neighbour simulation, never by argument.} Both findings in
\S\ref{sec:privacy} survived a convincing derivation and died on explicit construction.
\item \textbf{At $\delta$ scale, compute exactly rather than simulate.} A Monte Carlo run suggested
a $1.4\times$ violation that was pure noise; resolving that at $\delta=10^{-6}$ needs $\sim10^8$
trials.
\item \textbf{Check the grid before believing a negative.} An ``oracle bounds'' comparison on a
$2\times$-spaced grid --- coarser than the $1.3\times$ error basin --- reported our own arm getting
worse under perfect bounds. Pure artifact.
\end{itemize}

\section{Other ideas we tried}\label{sec:other}

Four other directions were evaluated at length. None is recommended as a headline contribution,
for different reasons.

\paragraph{$\ell_1$ per-unit norm clip.} Bound a unit's \emph{total} across groups rather than each
cell, giving sensitivity $B$ independent of how many groups it touches. Against the published
baseline it is worth $3.3$--$3.6\times$ within its scope, $1.23\times$ in median across all
queries; against a baseline hardened with unpublished improvements it is a wash ($1.01\times$). The
scope condition is measurable in advance from the $\ku$ histogram: it needs units touching
$\gtrsim 10$ groups, and it \emph{loses} by up to $1.27\times$ when $\ku$ is bimodal, because a few
wide-spreading units set $B$ for everybody. Its real argument is not utility but parameter
elimination --- it has no $\Cv$ to select at all.

\paragraph{Frozen partition set.} Release the group set once and reuse it, freeing $\epsilon_\eta$
for the values. This works well ($1.2$--$4.1\times$, up to $8.2\times$ where $\tau$ binds hard) and
helps \emph{any} mechanism, ours or the baseline's. It is however prior art: Tumult Analytics ships
\texttt{get\_groups} + \texttt{KeySet}, and Privacy on Beam documents deriving partitions via
\texttt{SelectPartitions} and passing them as public partitions. What remains unclaimed is narrow
--- a \emph{persistent} catalogue reused across separately-submitted queries rather than within one
pipeline. One caveat found late: freezing \emph{hurts} on unfiltered queries, where the frozen set
is the query's own set and the amortised cost buys nothing.

\paragraph{Gaussian partition selection.} Votes have $\ell_1 = \ku$ but $\ell_2 = \sqrt{\ku}$, so
Gaussian noise avoids truncation entirely and converts queries that return nothing into ones
returning $94\%$ of their key set. Comprehensively pre-empted: implemented in Google's C++
(\texttt{GaussianPartitionSelection}), OpenDP, PipelineDP and Qrlew, and Desfontaines et al.\
(PoPETs 2022) publish the crossover at $\kappa = 3$ with $\epsilon/\delta$-invariance --- we
independently measured $\ku \approx 4.3$ with the same invariance, which is a rediscovery.

\paragraph{All-or-Frozen thresholding.} Replace $K$ per-group release tests with one global AND,
saving $\approx(\Cu/\epsilon_B)\log\Cu$ on the threshold. The privacy analysis is correct --- we
reproduced the predicted gap to the digit --- but the rule essentially never fires. Any valid
threshold satisfies $\Pr[1+\mathrm{Lap}\ge\tau] \le \delta$, so $\tau \gg 1$ and a group holding a
single unit can never pass; five of eight tested queries have minimum support exactly $1$. But
single-unit groups are precisely why partition selection exists. \textbf{The mechanism requires a
query with no singleton group, and such a query does not need partition selection at all.} Two
findings survive: reusing the bound-selection histogram costs $736\times$ on the threshold versus a
dedicated count (a free statistic is not free when it is the wrong statistic), and blocking the
conjunction by a unit-determined key component is a valid way to shrink any per-query AND.

\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{The primitive is standard.} Steps 3--4 are an exponential-mechanism
quantile~\cite{mcsherry2007}. Gillenwater et al.~\cite{gillenwater2021} state exactly this
rank-distance utility and prove its $L_1$ sensitivity is $1$ (their Lemma 7), and attribute the
single-quantile algorithm to Smith~\cite{smith2011}, noting it ``has since been incorporated into
the SmartNoise and IBM differential privacy libraries''. It ships in SmartNoise and IBM diffprivlib. \textbf{We claim nothing here}, and
in particular the observation that the exponential mechanism needs only \emph{score} sensitivity
rather than the sensitivity of the underlying statistic is the defining property of that mechanism,
not an insight.

\paragraph{The principle is published.} Amin et al.~\cite{amin2019} prove that the optimal
contribution cap \emph{is} a quantile of the per-user contribution distribution and state
explicitly that it must itself be computed privately, citing the DP quantile literature. They leave
instantiating it as an open question. Their $x_i$ is records per user --- a volume bound.

\paragraph{What is selected elsewhere is a different parameter.} The literature contains three
distinct per-user bounds, all called ``contribution bounds'', and the distinction is not cosmetic:
for SUM queries a user may have $\ell_0 = 3$ and $\ell_1 = 10^6$.

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
bound & meaning & Google's name \\
\midrule
$\ell_0$ & how many distinct groups one user may touch & \verb|max_partitions_contributed| ($\Cu$) \\
$\ell_\infty$ & within one group & \verb|max_contributions_per_partition| \\
$\ell_1$ & one user's total across all groups & --- \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The two closest works select the others. \cite{click2024} (\S4.4--4.5) uses the \emph{identical} EM
quantile with the \emph{identical} utility function, and even freezes the result with a
sparse-vector refresh --- but its Theorem 4.2 sums $r_i^2/\sigma_i^2$ over all $n$ days with no cap
on how many days a user spans, and its Definition 4.1 is ``Bounded \emph{Per-day} Contributions'':
$r_i$ is an $\ell_\infty$ bound, and that mechanism has no $\ell_0$ bound at all. Liu et
al.~\cite{liu2023} do use the exponential mechanism to select a bound that constrains $\ell_0$ --- but
as a by-product of $\ell_1$ item-sampling (\texttt{rand-clip} draws $\min(\|N\|_1, C)$ items, so
``each $\|h_i\|_r \le C$ for $r = 0,1,2,\infty$''), only for their unbounded-domain sparse-vector
algorithm, and in a COUNT setting where $\ell_0 = \ell_1$ numerically. Their score is expected
release error at sensitivity $5C_m/2$, not a rank distance at sensitivity $1$. Google's
DP-SQLP~\cite{dpsqlp2024} recommends the ``99th percentile of per-user records… chosen in a DP way''
--- records again.

\paragraph{The nearest analogue in learning} is adaptive clipping~\cite{andrew2021}, which clips to
a privately estimated quantile of the gradient-norm distribution, tracked online rather than by
exponential mechanism. Same idea one parameter over: an $\ell_2$ magnitude bound, not a span bound.

\paragraph{One system does select $\ell_0$ privately, and it is the closest prior art.}
PipelineDP ships an experimental \texttt{PrivateL0Calculator}~\cite{pipelinedp_l0} whose docstring
reads \emph{``Calculates differentially-private l0 bound (i.e.\ max\_partitions\_contributed)''}
and which applies an exponential mechanism over candidate bounds. \textbf{The mechanism is
therefore not new.} Two differences remain, and only the first is substantive:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{A different objective.} Their score is a bias/variance tradeoff,
$\mathrm{score}(k) = -\tfrac12 P\sigma - \tfrac12 \sum_u \max(\min(k_u, B) - k, 0)$ --- noise
impact plus dropped-data impact --- evaluated against the full contribution histogram. Ours targets
a \emph{rank}. That is not merely cosmetic: their score needs $\sigma$, $P$ and a public upper
bound $B$ as inputs, and its sensitivity depends on $B$, whereas a rank-distance score has
sensitivity $1$ unconditionally. The preliminary stress probe in \S8 includes their objective, but
a calibrated head-to-head across budgets, public-domain tightness and aggregates remains open.
\item \textbf{Scope.} Their docstring restricts it to \emph{``COUNT and PRIVACY\_ID\_COUNT
aggregations''}; per the accompanying documentation it is opt-in, Beam/Local only, requires a
pre-supplied partition list, sits outside the budget accountant, and is not invoked by the default
aggregation path.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{What is left.} Not the mechanism. What survives is (i) integration --- selection wired
into a SQL compiler's default path and charged to one budget, so an analyst never supplies
$\Cu$; (ii) the rank-based score and its unconditional sensitivity; and (iii) \textbf{the
calibration results of \S3}, which are what a practitioner actually needs and which no prior work
reports: the target fraction, its interaction with a shared $\Cu$, the freeze-versus-re-select
rule, and the finding that the whole approach inverts under smooth sensitivity. Google's own Plume
paper~\cite{plume2022} frames the general problem as open --- \emph{``tuning the contribution bound
$L$ can be critical to achieving good performance''}, tuned there by hand --- and Wilson et
al.~\cite{wilson2020} take $\Cu$ as a query literal.

\paragraph{Adjacent.} Reuse of a released bound is free by post-processing; caching DP
\emph{answers} across a workload is Turbo~\cite{turbo2023}. Tumult~\cite{tumult2022} ships
DP-derived-then-reused partition sets, which is prior art for the frozen key set discussed in
\S\ref{sec:other}. Partition selection itself~\cite{gopi2020,desfontaines2022} is a separate
channel; \cite{desfontaines2022} in particular publishes the Gaussian/Laplace crossover that our own
measurement in \S\ref{sec:other} independently rediscovered.

\section{Future work}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Compiler-derived public candidate domains.} The proof requires a public
$K \ge \max_u \ku$; using the observed maximum makes the output support data-dependent. A SQL
compiler should derive a valid domain from grouping-column types, \texttt{CHECK} constraints,
foreign keys, functional dependencies, public partition catalogues and bounded date ranges. The
compiler must also expose when it cannot prove a useful bound. This is necessary for genuinely
automatic selection: replacing one analyst-supplied $\Cu$ with an equally opaque $K$ is not enough.

\item \textbf{Separate existence and value bounds.} Partition release and value release pay for
different objects. A bound $C_e$ controls how many groups one unit may name and therefore the
partition threshold $\tau$; a possibly different $C_v$ controls truncation bias and value noise.
Selecting $(C_e,C_v)$ jointly may dominate the single \texttt{max\_partitions\_contributed} used by
current interfaces. The open technical problem is a private joint score with a useful sensitivity
bound, not the non-private grid search used in this report.

\item \textbf{Loss-aware selection for general aggregates.} PipelineDP's private $\ell_0$
calculator is documented for \texttt{COUNT} and privacy-ID count. An automatic SQL system must
handle \texttt{SUM}, \texttt{AVG} and multiple aggregates sharing one partition-selection channel.
The score should predict downstream query loss, including clipping bias and aggregate noise, while
remaining private; simply evaluating true query error on each candidate is an oracle, not a
deployable selector. The smooth-sensitivity path needs its own objective: the preliminary port in
this report shows that the Laplace calibration can invert there.

\item \textbf{Joint bound and budget optimisation.} The deployed choice is not $\Cu$ alone but
$(C_e,C_v,\epsilon_b,\epsilon_\eta,\epsilon_v)$. Our experiments tune these parameters with access
to the private truth. Future work should replace that oracle with one accounted private selection
step and determine whether the extra choice complexity consumes more privacy than it saves in
query error.

\item \textbf{A workload-level bound catalogue.} A selected bound should carry its privacy-unit
lineage, grouping lineage, predicate assumptions and privacy provenance. Static analysis can reuse
it only when those obligations still hold; a filter on a privacy-unit attribute does not in general
preserve the $\ku$ distribution. Where invariance cannot be proved, a sparse-vector-style drift
monitor could refresh metadata only after a material change. Scheduled refresh remains the
baseline: $R$ releases cost up to $R\delta_0$, whether or not the data changed.

\item \textbf{Tail-robust private selection.} A dense public domain can contain thousands of
candidates above the observed maximum. When those candidates have equal or nearly equal rank
score, their combined probability can dominate the exponential mechanism even though each is
individually poor. Candidate lattices, base measures or alternative ordered-selection mechanisms
should be evaluated with a requirement that widening a valid public domain does not radically
inflate the selected bound.

\item \textbf{Privacy-certified compilation.} An \texttt{EXPLAIN PRIVACY} mode should report the
adjacency relation, derived candidate domain, selected or reused $(C_e,C_v)$, sensitivity of every
release, partition-selection threshold, metadata provenance and the complete budget split. This
turns automatic selection into an auditable compiler decision rather than hidden preprocessing.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Preliminary mechanism-only probe.} We added
\texttt{attacks/automatic\_cu\_future.py} to test the non-compiler parts on privacy-ID
\texttt{COUNT} over the real TPC-H \texttt{month|nation} incidence relation. The selector uses the
public group-domain bound $K=2095$, not the observed maximum; utility configurations are chosen on
four simulation draws and reported on twelve independent draws. ``Loss oracle'' therefore measures
headroom, not a deployable private selector:

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{rrrrrr}
\toprule
units & observed max & original: $\Pr[\hat C>\max\ku]$ & direct+hybrid & shared oracle & separate oracle \\
\midrule
181,532 & 72 & 97.4\% & 4.5\% & 5.52\% & \textbf{3.82\%} \\
45,320  & 66 & 99.2\% & 69.2\% & 78.48\% & \textbf{41.56\%} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Removing the noisy-$N$ margin and replacing the dense upper tail with a fixed hybrid lattice solve
the large-population failure but not the smaller one: at $p=0.99$ and $0.008$ selection budget,
candidate multiplicity still overwhelms the rank gap. Separate bounds show real headroom in both
cases (1.45$\times$ and 1.89$\times$ over the shared loss oracle), which justifies pursuing a
private joint score; the high absolute error in the smaller population also shows that automatic
parameter selection cannot repair a partition-selection channel with inadequate support.

All seven directions are system or calibration problems, not claims that the underlying
exponential-mechanism quantile is new. The first integrated implementation should include the two
safety modifications from \S\ref{sec:privacy} and must be evaluated through compiled SQL rather
than simulation alone.
