\section{Motivation}

\subsection{The gap between a DP library and a DP system}

Differentially private aggregation is solved as an \emph{algorithm} and unsolved as a
\emph{product}. Every mature library --- Google DP, OpenDP, Tumult, PipelineDP --- can answer
\texttt{SELECT g, SUM(v) FROM T GROUP BY g} with a rigorous user-level guarantee. But none of them
can answer it \emph{without a human first supplying constants that depend on the data}:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{How much can one user contribute to one group?} A clipping bound. Set it too low and
you destroy the signal; too high and you drown it in noise.
\item \textbf{How many groups can one user touch?} \texttt{max\_partitions\_contributed}, called
$\Cu$ here. A required input in Google DP, with no default.
\end{itemize}

Our system rewrites ordinary SQL into a private plan automatically. An analyst writes a query; they
do not know --- and should not need to know --- the maximum number of months any customer appears
in. So every one of these constants is a blocker, and the question driving this work is narrow and
practical: \textbf{can $\Cu$ be chosen from the data itself, privately, without spending so much
budget that the answer becomes useless?}

\subsection{Guessing is not a safe fallback}

Two measured facts rule it out. The penalty is \emph{severely asymmetric} --- setting $\Cu$ too
small costs up to $38\times$ in error while setting it too large costs at most $1.7\times$ --- and
no single value works across datasets:

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{lrrrrr}
\toprule
analyst supplies & $\Cu{=}1$ & $\Cu{=}10$ & $\Cu{=}100$ & $\Cu{=}$ schema max & automatic \\
\midrule
worst case over queries & 177.6$\times$ & 136.3$\times$ & 5.18$\times$ & 4.18$\times$ &
\textbf{1.36$\times$} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

$\Cu{=}1$ is catastrophic on TPC-H and simultaneously \emph{optimal} on ClickBench. A single
constant cannot serve both, so the choice must adapt to the data --- which means spending privacy
budget to make it. The mechanism below spends about $1\%$ of $\epsilon$ doing so.

\subsection{Where the budget goes}

A grouped DP query spends privacy budget on three things:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Bound selection} ($\epsilon_b$): how large is one user's contribution?
\item \textbf{Partition selection} ($\epsilon_\eta$): which groups may be named at all? A group
existing because of a single user must not be revealed, so groups are released only when a noised
count of contributing users clears a threshold $\tau$.
\item \textbf{Values} ($\epsilon_v$): the noised aggregates themselves.
\end{enumerate}

The third is what the analyst wants; the first two are overhead.

\paragraph{How the baseline divides it.} Google DP charges these separately, though not as three
co-equal shares. Privacy on Beam splits the top level \emph{two} ways --- \texttt{count.go} exposes
\texttt{AggregationEpsilon} and \texttt{PartitionSelectionEpsilon} as distinct fields, consumed by
distinct calls --- and bound selection sits \emph{inside} the aggregation: \texttt{ApproxBounds} is
constructed with its own \texttt{epsilon} taken from the aggregation's share. We model all three as
a single three-way split and tune it jointly for every arm, which is at least as favourable to the
baseline as its own nested structure, since a nested split cannot beat a free one.

\paragraph{Scoring.} Throughout: relative $\ell_1$ error over the \emph{true, uncapped} key set,
with $\tau$-suppressed groups charged as released $0$ (full error). Every arm is tuned separately
over its own free parameters. Anything less lets a mechanism buy accuracy by returning fewer rows,
or flatters one side by fixing a parameter the other gets to optimise.

\section{The mechanism}

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Core idea.} The bound we need is a property of one \emph{distribution} --- how many groups
each user touches. Rather than measuring that distribution privately and reading a bound off it, we
turn the problem into a \emph{choice among candidate bounds}, scored by a quantity that a single
user can move by at most one. Choosing is far cheaper than measuring, and the asymmetry of the
error curve tells us which way to lean when the choice is imperfect.
\end{quote}

\subsection{In words}

Ask each user how many groups they appear in. On a monthly sales query one customer might appear in
30 months, another in 3, a few in 70. Lay those counts out as a distribution and $\Cu$ is a cut
point on it: choose it, and every user above the line has their extra groups discarded.

The mechanism sets that cut point at a \emph{quantile} --- ``pick $\Cu$ so that $95\%$ of users are
left untouched'' --- and it does so privately, because the distribution is itself sensitive data.
Three design choices make this work.

\paragraph{Aim at a rank, not a value.} Rather than asking ``what is the largest $\ku$?'', which one
user can move arbitrarily, we ask ``at what cut point do we reach $95\%$ of users?''. That target is
a \emph{count} of users, and one user changes any count by at most one. The bound is thus pinned to
a robust feature of the distribution, not to its most extreme member.

\paragraph{Choose, do not measure.} The obvious approach is to release the whole distribution
noisily and then read the quantile off it. That is expensive: one user with $\ku = 1$ shifts every
cumulative count at once, so publishing the full curve requires noise proportional to its length.
Instead we never publish the curve. We \emph{score} each candidate cut point by how close it lands
to the target rank, and draw one candidate at random with better candidates exponentially more
likely. Only the winner is released. Since each individual candidate's score moves by at most one
when a user is added or removed, the whole selection costs the same as measuring a single count ---
about $1\%$ of the query's budget in practice.

\paragraph{Deliberately overshoot.} A quantile is only a proxy for the bound that actually minimises
error, so the two will not coincide. That is tolerable because the error curve is lopsided:
undershooting discards real user data and costs up to $35\times$, while overshooting merely adds
noise and costs at most $2\times$ (\S\ref{sec:asym}). The target fraction $p$ is therefore set high
--- $0.95$ or $0.99$ rather than the $0.7$ one might first reach for --- so the mechanism errs on
the safe side of a curve that punishes one direction far more than the other.

Because the resulting bound depends only on the shape of the $\ku$ distribution, and that shape is
stable under filters on the user (\S\ref{sec:freeze}), the released value can be frozen and reused
across many later queries at no further cost.

\subsection{Formally}

Let $\ku = |\{g : u \text{ contributes to } g\}|$ and $F_d(r) = |\{u : \ku \le r\}|$, the number of
users whose group count is at most $r$. Given a public upper bound $K \ge \ku$ and a target
fraction $p$:

\begin{enumerate}
\item Release $\tilde N = N + \mathrm{Lap}(1/\epsilon_N)$, the number of users, at cost
$(\epsilon_N, 0)$. Its sensitivity is $1$.
\item Form a conservative target rank $T = p\tilde N + (p/\epsilon_N)\ln(1/2\beta_N)$; the margin
ensures $T \ge pN$ with probability $1-\beta_N$, so noise in $\tilde N$ does not silently pull the
target below its intended quantile.
\item Score each candidate $r \in \{1,\dots,K\}$ by $q(d,r) = -|F_d(r) - T|$: a candidate scores
well when the number of users it leaves untruncated is close to the target.
\item Sample by exponential mechanism, $\Pr[\hat\Cu = r] \propto \exp(\epsilon_C\, q(d,r)/2)$, at
cost $(\epsilon_C, 0)$. The histogram, the cumulative counts and the scores all remain internal;
only $\hat\Cu$ is released.
\end{enumerate}

Total $(\epsilon_N + \epsilon_C, 0)$-DP by adaptive sequential composition. $\hat\Cu$ is then
frozen and reused as public metadata.

\subsection{Why the score sensitivity is $1$, not $K$}

This is what makes step 4 cheap. Removing one user with $\ku = k$ shifts $K-k+1$ cumulative counts
\emph{simultaneously}, so \emph{releasing} the vector $(F(1),\dots,F(K))$ would have $\ell_1$
sensitivity up to $K$. But the exponential mechanism never releases that vector --- it scores each
candidate separately, and what matters is $\Delta q = \max_r |q(d,r) - q(d',r)|$. Verified by
explicit construction:

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{lrrr}
\toprule
removed unit & cumulative counts changed & $\ell_1$ change & $\max_r |\Delta q|$ \\
\midrule
$\ku = 1$  & 80 of 80 & 80 & \textbf{1.0} \\
$\ku = 30$ & 51 of 80 & 51 & \textbf{1.0} \\
$\ku = 70$ & 11 of 80 & 11 & \textbf{1.0} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The $\ell_1$ of the histogram is a red herring: one cheap release replaces $K$ noisy counts.

\section{Results}

\subsection{The selection is nearly free and essentially deterministic}

With $\sim$180k units, $F$ jumps by thousands between adjacent candidates, so the exponential
mechanism's distribution is sharply peaked. All 15 draws agree at every budget tested, and
\emph{spending more actively hurts}, because it comes out of the query:

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{lrrrr}
\toprule
$\epsilon_N + \epsilon_C$ & 0.010 & 0.025 & 0.100 & 0.250 \\
\midrule
selected $\Cu$, 15 draws & 37--37 & 37--37 & 37--37 & 37--37 \\
penalty vs oracle $\Cu$ & \textbf{1.00$\times$} & 1.00$\times$ & 1.04$\times$ & 1.16$\times$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\textbf{About $1\%$ of $\epsilon$ is the right budget.}

\subsection{Freeze once, or re-select per query?}\label{sec:freeze}

A single frozen $\Cu$ suffices for filters on the \emph{privacy unit}; only group-removing filters
justify re-selection. Frozen $\Cu = 48$ from the unfiltered query; per-query re-runs the mechanism
and pays $0.01$ again each time:

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{lrrrrrr}
\toprule
query & units & $\ku$ p50 & p95 & per-query $\Cu$ & frozen & per-query \\
\midrule
PU-side: acctbal $\ge$ 8000 & 181,532 & 30 & 48 & 48 & \textbf{12.36\%} & 12.56\% \\
PU-side: acctbal $\ge$ 9500 &  45,320 & 30 & 48 & 53 & \textbf{85.14\%} & 87.16\% \\
PU-side: acctbal $\ge$ 9900 &   9,182 & 30 & 48 & 57 & 100.0\% & 99.99\% \\
GRP-side: ship $\ge$ 1998   & 821,108 & \textbf{4} & \textbf{8} & \textbf{8} & 2.52\% & \textbf{1.38\%} \\
GRP-side: nation $<$ 5      & 199,738 & 30 & 48 & 48 & \textbf{2.31\%} & 2.40\% \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

A filter on the privacy unit removes users, but the survivors still touch the same number of groups
--- the $\ku$ distribution is \emph{identical} (p50 $=30$, p95 $=48$) even at a filter retaining
9,182 of 1,000,000 users. Re-selection returns the same value and is slightly worse for paying its
cost again. A filter that removes \emph{groups} collapses $\ku$ (p50 $30\to4$), and there
re-selection wins $1.8\times$.

Note that \texttt{nation < 5} constrains the grouping key yet behaves like a unit-side filter,
because nation is functionally determined by the customer. The decidable rule is therefore:

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Re-run the selection when the filter constrains a grouping-key component that is \emph{not}
functionally determined by the privacy unit. Otherwise reuse the frozen value.}
\end{quote}

\section{Privacy validation}

Beyond the $\Delta q \le 1$ construction above, we ran an explicit adversary against the full
pipeline, $4\times10^6$ trials per attack, measuring the advantage
$\Pr[\text{say } D'\,|\,D'] - \Pr[\text{say } D'\,|\,D]$ against the ceiling
$(e^\epsilon-1)/(e^\epsilon+1)$.

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
attack & result & verdict \\
\midrule
value channel, worst-case target & advantage 0.2587 vs bound 0.2913, \emph{scale-free} & pass \\
omniscient attacker (knows all other units) & 0.2590 even at a target contributing $4B$ & pass \\
reconstruction of a target's value & posterior sd 1,167 vs prior 1,183 ($1.01\times$) & pass \\
reuse of one frozen set over $N$ queries & advantage tracks composed $\epsilon_v$ only & pass \\
adaptive: round 1 picks the round-2 target & \emph{loses}, $0.36\times$ to $0.01\times$ & pass \\
SQL join fan-out (182 rows per unit) & $\|\Delta\|_1 = 1.000000\,B$ exactly & pass \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Two bugs were found in \emph{our own} mechanisms, both by explicit neighbour construction after the
argument had already convinced us.

\paragraph{Bug 1: a clip that was not a norm.} Written as $n_u = \sum_g \min(t,B)$ it is a signed
sum, which fails open on signed measures. On \texttt{SUM(shipped - returned)} at sf10,
\textbf{393,053 of 999,982 units exceeded the bound}, worst $2.6\times$ --- making the release
$2.6\epsilon$-DP. Fixed by $n_u := \sum_g|\mathrm{clip}(t,-B,B)|$, bit-identical on non-negative
data.

\paragraph{Bug 2: $\delta$ under-charged, demonstrated by an adversary.} A unit creates a group by
having a \emph{value} there, not a vote, so with votes truncated to $\Ce$ the $\ku-\Ce$ unvoted
groups each get an independent chance to clear $\tau$ on noise alone --- chances $\tau$'s union
bound never covered:

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{rrrrr}
\toprule
$\Ce$ & $\ku$ & leak, no gate & leak, gated & over by \\
\midrule
1 & 30 & $2.10\times10^{-5}$ & $1.00\times10^{-6}$ & \lose 21.0$\times$ \\
1 & 72 & $4.70\times10^{-5}$ & $1.25\times10^{-6}$ & \lose \textbf{47.0$\times$} \\
5 & 72 & $1.27\times10^{-5}$ & $1.75\times10^{-6}$ & \lose 12.8$\times$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The fix is one line --- release a group only if it has at least one vote --- and it is free: on real
data it removes 19 of 2,095 groups, each with a $2\times10^{-7}$ chance of release.

\paragraph{Refreshes must be budgeted.} $R$ re-releases of frozen metadata give $R$ independent
chances: $\Pr[\text{exposed}] = 1-(1-p)^R \le R\,\delta_0$, confirmed exactly at $R = 1..50$.
Batching updates does not reduce the cost of a single refresh; it reduces $R$.

\section{Methodology}\label{sec:method}

Nine headline numbers were produced and then invalidated during this work
($880\times \to 12\times \to 4.8\times \to 4.65\times \to 1.36\times$, and separately
$4.5\times \to 1.9\times$). Every collapse had one of three causes, and the pattern is more
transferable than any of the numbers.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{An untuned baseline.} Comparisons at a fixed budget split, a fixed $\Cu$, or against
a library default are not evidence. $\Cu$ tuning alone moved one result by $12\times$.
\item \textbf{A forgiving scoring rule.} Scoring against a capped or truncated ``truth'' silently
forgives exactly the bias being measured. Suppressed groups must be charged full error.
\item \textbf{Mixing regimes in an average.} A median across queries that violate a mechanism's
scope condition misleads in both directions. Report by regime.
\end{enumerate}

Three practices proved necessary:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Verify sensitivity by neighbour simulation, never by argument.} Both privacy bugs
survived a convincing derivation and died on explicit construction.
\item \textbf{At $\delta$ scale, compute exactly rather than simulate.} A Monte Carlo run suggested
a $1.4\times$ violation that was pure noise; resolving that at $\delta=10^{-6}$ needs $\sim10^8$
trials.
\item \textbf{Check the grid before believing a negative.} An ``oracle bounds'' comparison on a
$2\times$-spaced grid --- coarser than the $1.3\times$ error basin --- reported our own arm getting
worse under perfect bounds. Pure artifact.
\end{itemize}

\section{Other ideas we tried}

Four other directions were evaluated at length. None is recommended as a headline contribution,
for different reasons.

\paragraph{$\ell_1$ per-unit norm clip.} Bound a unit's \emph{total} across groups rather than each
cell, giving sensitivity $B$ independent of how many groups it touches. Against the published
baseline it is worth $3.3$--$3.6\times$ within its scope, $1.23\times$ in median across all
queries; against a baseline hardened with unpublished improvements it is a wash ($1.01\times$). The
scope condition is measurable in advance from the $\ku$ histogram: it needs units touching
$\gtrsim 10$ groups, and it \emph{loses} by up to $1.27\times$ when $\ku$ is bimodal, because a few
wide-spreading units set $B$ for everybody. Its real argument is not utility but parameter
elimination --- it has no $\Cv$ to select at all.

\paragraph{Frozen partition set.} Release the group set once and reuse it, freeing $\epsilon_\eta$
for the values. This works well ($1.2$--$4.1\times$, up to $8.2\times$ where $\tau$ binds hard) and
helps \emph{any} mechanism, ours or the baseline's. It is however prior art: Tumult Analytics ships
\texttt{get\_groups} + \texttt{KeySet}, and Privacy on Beam documents deriving partitions via
\texttt{SelectPartitions} and passing them as public partitions. What remains unclaimed is narrow
--- a \emph{persistent} catalogue reused across separately-submitted queries rather than within one
pipeline. One caveat found late: freezing \emph{hurts} on unfiltered queries, where the frozen set
is the query's own set and the amortised cost buys nothing.

\paragraph{Gaussian partition selection.} Votes have $\ell_1 = \ku$ but $\ell_2 = \sqrt{\ku}$, so
Gaussian noise avoids truncation entirely and converts queries that return nothing into ones
returning $94\%$ of their key set. Comprehensively pre-empted: implemented in Google's C++
(\texttt{GaussianPartitionSelection}), OpenDP, PipelineDP and Qrlew, and Desfontaines et al.\
(PoPETs 2022) publish the crossover at $\kappa = 3$ with $\epsilon/\delta$-invariance --- we
independently measured $\ku \approx 4.3$ with the same invariance, which is a rediscovery.

\paragraph{All-or-Frozen thresholding.} Replace $K$ per-group release tests with one global AND,
saving $\approx(\Cu/\epsilon_B)\log\Cu$ on the threshold. The privacy analysis is correct --- we
reproduced the predicted gap to the digit --- but the rule essentially never fires. Any valid
threshold satisfies $\Pr[1+\mathrm{Lap}\ge\tau] \le \delta$, so $\tau \gg 1$ and a group holding a
single unit can never pass; five of eight tested queries have minimum support exactly $1$. But
single-unit groups are precisely why partition selection exists. \textbf{The mechanism requires a
query with no singleton group, and such a query does not need partition selection at all.} Two
findings survive: reusing the bound-selection histogram costs $736\times$ on the threshold versus a
dedicated count (a free statistic is not free when it is the wrong statistic), and blocking the
conjunction by a unit-determined key component is a valid way to shrink any per-query AND.

\section{Open questions}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Prior art for the selection mechanism is being checked.} Our earlier prior-art sweep
covered the four ideas in \S6 and found four of five techniques already exist. The
exponential-mechanism selection of $\Cu$ was proposed later and is going through that process now.
The nearest neighbours to settle: Amin et al., ``Bounding User Contributions'' (ICML 2019);
``Algorithms for bounding contribution for histogram estimation under user-level privacy''
(arXiv:2206.03008); Andrew et al., ``Differentially Private Learning with Adaptive Clipping''
(NeurIPS 2021), which estimates a norm quantile online under DP; and the DP-quantile literature
generally, since steps 3--4 are an exponential-mechanism quantile. \textbf{This should be settled
before any claim of novelty.}
\item \textbf{Nothing is implemented.} All results are simulation. Both privacy fixes must land
\emph{with} the mechanism, not after.
\item \textbf{Multi-aggregate queries dilute the effect.} Every headline here is single-aggregate;
with four aggregates the advantage of the clip decays from $3.48\times$ to $1.96\times$ as
$\epsilon_v$ splits and the shared $\tau$ cost is amortised.
\item \textbf{The smooth-sensitivity path is untested.} A preliminary port \emph{lost}, because
that value channel is independently broken --- releasing more groups makes it worse.
\item \textbf{Drift-triggered rather than scheduled refreshes.} Since refreshes compose linearly, a
monitor that re-releases only on genuine drift (a sparse-vector construction, paying per positive
rather than per check) is the natural next step for frozen metadata.
\end{enumerate}
